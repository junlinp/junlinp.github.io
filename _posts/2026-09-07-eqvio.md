Reading notes on van Goor and Mahony, ["EqVIO: An Equivariant Filter for Visual Inertial Odometry"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01980) (IEEE Transactions on Robotics, vol. 39, no. 5, 2023; arXiv:2205.01980). Third post in this series: [the general EqF/EqF⋆ theory](/technical/2026/09/06/equivariant-filter.html) built the machinery, [the INS post](/technical/2026/09/06/equivariant-symmetries-ins.html) applied it to IMU+GNSS, and this paper applies the whole toolkit to full monocular visual-inertial odometry — pose, velocity, IMU biases, camera extrinsics, *and* a live map of visual landmarks, all estimated jointly.

This is the hardest system in the series so far, and the paper's central argument is worth stating up front: standard EKF-based VIO (VINS-Mono, ROVIO, MSCKF-style filters) has a *structural* consistency problem, not just a linearization-quality one. VIO has a real, physical unobservable subspace — you can rotate the whole trajectory about the gravity direction and translate it arbitrarily, and no combination of IMU + camera measurements can tell the difference. A generic EKF's linearized error model doesn't automatically respect that symmetry, so it ends up *falsely observing* directions that are actually unobservable — which is exactly what makes EKF-VIO covariances overconfident in practice. EqVIO's symmetry is deliberately built so the linearized system inherits the same unobservable subspace as the real one, by construction.

## The state

The full VI-SLAM state bundles four things: the standard IMU navigation state, IMU biases, the camera-to-IMU extrinsic calibration, and $n$ visual landmarks:

$$
\xi = (\xi_B,\, b_B,\, T,\, p_1,\ldots,p_n), \qquad \xi_B = (R_B, x_B, v_B) \in SE_2(3), \quad b_B\in\mathbb{R}^6, \quad T\in SE(3), \quad p_i\in\mathbb{R}^3
$$

$\xi\_B$ is the by-now-familiar extended pose (attitude, position, velocity) from the [INS post](/technical/2026/09/06/equivariant-symmetries-ins.html)'s $G\_{ES}$ symmetry; $b\_B$ is the gyro+accel bias pair; $T$ is the fixed (but unknown, to be calibrated online) transform from the IMU frame to the camera frame; and each $p\_i\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is a landmark position in the inertial frame. This is, structurally, "IMU navigation state, plus a SLAM map, plus an extrinsic calibration" — three separate estimation problems usually handled with three separate tricks, here unified under one symmetry group.

## Bias-free IMU dynamics are group-affine — a stronger property than mere equivariance

The paper leans on a fact due to Barrau and Bonnabel: with the bias terms removed, the IMU dynamics

$$
\dot R_B = R_B\Omega^\wedge, \qquad \dot x_B = v_B, \qquad \dot v_B = R_Ba + g\mathbf{e}_3
$$

aren't just equivariant with respect to the $SE\_2(3)$ action $\phi(A,\xi\_B):=(R\_BR\_A,\,x\_B+R\_Bx\_A,\,v\_B+R\_Bv\_A)$ — they're **group-affine**: the dynamics, expressed in the group's own coordinates, are literally affine (linear-plus-constant) in the state, with no higher-order terms left over at all. That's a strictly stronger property than the general EqF setup requires, and it has a correspondingly stronger payoff: propagating the covariance in these coordinates is *exact*, not first-order — the "linearization error" during propagation is genuinely zero, and the only place approximation error can creep back in is through the biases (which spoil the group-affine property, since they don't transform under the same $SE\_2(3)$ action) and the measurement update. This is the IMU-side analogue of what the previous post's $G\_{TG}$ symmetry bought for plain INS — except here it's not a design trick, it's a pre-existing exact property of the bias-free dynamics that the symmetry is chosen to preserve.

## Landmarks: a polar parametrization built for the symmetry

Rather than track each landmark as a raw 3D position (which doesn't transform cleanly under the navigation symmetry above), the paper introduces landmark coordinates matched to a group $\mathbf{SOT}(3)$ — a "scaled orthogonal transform": rotation plus positive scale, exactly the $S^1\times\mathbb{R}\_{>0}$-type structure from the toy landmark example in the very first post of this series, just promoted to 3D. A landmark's position in the camera frame, $q\in\mathbb{R}^3\setminus\lbrace 0\rbrace$, gets re-expressed as a bearing angle (split into two components) plus a log-depth:

$$
\varsigma(q) := \left(\theta\,\frac{q_2}{\lVert\mathbf{e}_3\times q\rVert},\;\; \theta\,\frac{-q_1}{\lVert\mathbf{e}_3\times q\rVert},\;\; -\log\lVert q\rVert\right), \qquad \theta:=\arccos\!\left(\frac{q_3}{\lVert q\rVert}\right)
$$

This is a genuine generalization of the classical inverse-depth landmark parametrization used throughout monocular SLAM (the last coordinate is literally log-inverse-depth), reshaped so that a landmark's *scale* (depth) and *bearing* (direction) each transform simply — by translation in log-depth and by the group's rotation in bearing — under the $\mathbf{SOT}(3)$ action, which is exactly what a per-landmark equivariant lift needs.

## Deriving the visual measurement's equivariance, from scratch

This is the one piece worth carrying through by hand, since it's small, self-contained, and it's the mechanism that lets EqF⋆'s sharper output linearization (from [the general theory post](/technical/2026/09/06/equivariant-filter.html)) apply to VIO's actual camera measurements. A single landmark's bearing observation is $y = h(q) := q/\lVert q\rVert \in S^2$ — literally the same bearing-projection output map as the $S^2$ example from that post, just for a 3D landmark instead of a fixed inertial direction.

The claimed group action on the landmark (in camera-frame coordinates) and on the output space, for $Q=(R\_Q,c\_Q)\in\mathbf{SOT}(3)$:

$$
\varphi(Q,q) := c_Q^{-1}R_Q^\top q, \qquad \rho(Q,y) := R_Q^\top y
$$

Checking equivariance directly: since $c\_Q>0$, $\lVert c\_Q^{-1}R\_Q^\top q\rVert = c\_Q^{-1}\lVert R\_Q^\top q\rVert = c\_Q^{-1}\lVert q\rVert$ (rotations preserve norm), so

$$
h(\varphi(Q,q)) = \frac{c_Q^{-1}R_Q^\top q}{c_Q^{-1}\lVert q\rVert} = \frac{R_Q^\top q}{\lVert q\rVert} = R_Q^\top\left(\frac{q}{\lVert q\rVert}\right) = \rho(Q,h(q))
$$

exactly, with no approximation — the scale factor $c\_Q^{-1}$ cancels cleanly out of the normalization, leaving only the rotation. That's the whole content of the paper's Lemma 5.2, and it's what lets the higher-order EqF⋆ output correction (averaging the bearing's cross-product structure at the predicted *and* measured directions, exactly as derived in the general-theory post's $S^2$ example) carry over directly to real visual features, buying the same jump from second-order to third-order output linearization error for every landmark in the map.

## Reference-frame invariance, and why it's the real point

This is the paper's central conceptual contribution, and it's worth being upfront that I'm reporting the mechanism rather than re-deriving it — the exact bookkeeping of how it interacts with the full $SE\_2(3)\times\mathbb{R}^6\times SE(3)\times\mathbf{SOT}(3)^n$ symmetry is intricate enough that I'd rather explain it accurately than fake an independent derivation.

VIO's true, physical unobservable subspace is 4-dimensional: global position (3 DOF) and yaw — rotation about the gravity direction (1 DOF; pitch and roll *are* observable, because gravity gives the IMU an absolute reference for "down"). No amount of camera or IMU data can pin these down, because the whole problem is invariant under simultaneously rotating everything about vertical and translating it: rotate the trajectory, the camera poses, and every landmark together by the same yaw, and every relative-bearing and every inertial measurement looks identical.

The paper formalizes this as a group action $\alpha$ of $SE\_{\mathbf{e}\_3}(3)$ (rigid transforms that preserve the vertical direction — rotation about the gravity axis, plus arbitrary translation) on the *entire* state — pose, velocity, landmarks, everything gets rotated and translated together, while biases and the camera extrinsic (which are frame-independent) are left alone. The key result (its Lemma 5.4) is that this action *commutes* with the EqF's own symmetry action and lift: $\phi(X,\alpha(S,\xi)) = \alpha(S,\phi(X,\xi))$ for every $S$. That commuting property is what forces the linearized error dynamics to inherit exactly the same 4-dimensional unobservable subspace as the true nonlinear system — not approximately, but as an algebraic consequence of the symmetry having been built to respect it. A plain EKF's Jacobian, taken at an arbitrary operating point, has no reason to respect this structure at all, and generically won't — which is the paper's diagnosis for why standard EKF-VIO tends to be overconfident: it's quietly claiming information about directions that are, in fact, completely unobservable.

## Results

On EuRoC, EqVIO's mean position RMSE (~0.16 m) matches the best competing filter (OpenVINS) while running roughly 2× faster (~5.4 ms/frame vs. ~11.6 ms for OpenVINS, and far ahead of VINS-Mono's ~32 ms). On the harder UZH-FPV dataset (aggressive, high-rate drone flight), EqVIO again leads or ties on accuracy (~0.27 m mean RMSE) while running about 5× faster than the next-fastest competitor. Separately, a 1000-trial Monte Carlo consistency check on a synthetic trajectory shows the filter's reported normalized estimation error squared (NEES) tracking its theoretical $\chi^2$ distribution closely over a 200-second, 10-loop trajectory — direct evidence that the reference-frame-invariance argument above isn't just theoretically appealing, it measurably fixes the consistency problem in practice.

## Closing thoughts

Zooming out over the whole series: the first post built the general machinery and showed a toy example works out exactly by hand; the second showed that even within "valid equivariant filter" designs, the *choice* of symmetry group has real, measurable consequences (TG-EqF's exact navigation-state linearization translating directly into better consistency and faster convergence); this one shows the same lesson scales all the way up to a real, deployed, state-of-the-art VIO system — and adds a new dimension to it: the symmetry isn't just chosen to make the *dynamics* linearize nicely, it's chosen to make the *unobservable directions* line up correctly too. That's a genuinely different kind of design consideration than "does this reduce linearization error," and it's arguably the more important one for a filter that has to run reliably, for hours, without ever seeing an absolute position or heading reference.
